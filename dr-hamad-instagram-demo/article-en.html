Knee osteoarthritis:
from understanding pain
to treatment decisions.

Understanding what happens in the joint helps us ask an important question: how can we maintain movement, and when might a different treatment be considered?

Pain does not tell the whole story of the joint

“Cartilage wear” may sound like a simple explanation for knee osteoarthritis, but it does not capture the complexity of the condition. Osteoarthritis involves changes in the joint that can affect movement and symptoms to different degrees. The name of the condition alone does not explain a person’s experience, and a treatment plan cannot be based on an X-ray image alone. What pain prevents someone from doing in daily life is also an important part of assessment. [1]

A person may also think that a joint replacement removes the whole knee. In total knee replacement, the damaged joint surfaces are reshaped and covered with components: one on the end of the thigh bone, one on the top of the shin bone, and a liner between them that allows movement. The back of the kneecap may also be resurfaced in some cases. This helps explain the procedure, but does not by itself determine when someone needs it. [4]

Assessment starts with the person, then the image

A clinician may ask when the pain began, how it relates to activity, whether there is stiffness or swelling, and how symptoms affect walking, sleep, and work. In many cases, osteoarthritis can be diagnosed clinically in people aged 45 or older who have activity-related pain and no morning stiffness, or stiffness lasting no longer than 30 minutes. Atypical symptoms or concern about another cause may require imaging or further tests. These criteria support clinical assessment; they are not a home diagnostic test. [1]

Why does movement remain part of treatment?

Clinical guidance recommends therapeutic exercise tailored to the person. A Cochrane review published in December 2024 found that exercise may improve pain and function in the short term, with evidence certainty ranging from low to moderate. The review also noted that average improvements do not always reach the threshold for a clinically important difference. Exercise is therefore best understood as part of a plan that can be adjusted and monitored, rather than a promise of the same result for everyone. [2]

For people who are above a healthy weight, combining nutrition support with exercise may be helpful. The IDEA trial studied this approach in older adults with knee osteoarthritis and overweight or obesity. The diet-and-exercise group had better results on some pain and function measures than the exercise-only group. A study result should not be treated as a guarantee for every individual; a suitable plan considers health, preferences, and what a person can sustain. [3]

When does joint replacement enter the discussion?

It may be appropriate to discuss replacement when symptoms substantially affect quality of life and non-surgical options have not helped enough, or are unsuitable. The decision includes a conversation about likely benefits, risks, and expectations; it should not rely on the X-ray grade alone. Comparing implant brand names is also not enough to choose a component. Design and fixation relate to knee anatomy, the patient’s condition, and the surgeon’s assessment. [1] [4]

The practical message is that treatment is a path shaped by symptoms, function, and personal goals. Patients can start by understanding the problem, then work with their care team to choose the next step—whether adjusting the current plan or discussing surgery.
